The two searches catch different things. Keyword search looks for the exact words and deal names investigators would use. Meaning search (the semantic channel) finds emails that say similar things in different words. An email that both searches rank highly is a strong lead.

3. How the search works, in three steps

Step 1: A word list in four groups

From the proposal (Table 2), we built a list of 68 search terms in four groups. Each group answers a different question an investigator would ask:

A · Which deal?

Names of the partnerships: Raptor, LJM, Chewco, JEDI, Hawaii 125-0, Whitewing, Condor, Talon…

B · Is Enron still on the hook?

guarantee, recourse, repurchase, total return swap, collateral, Enron stock…

C · How was it accounted for?

off-balance-sheet, hedge, mark-to-market, write-down, disclosure, auditor…

D · Warning signs

backdate, shortfall, insufficient, do not disclose, leave out, quarter-end…

Some deal names are also ordinary words: Talon, Marlin and Osprey are also companies, birds or pipeline names. These only count when a finance word appears near them. Two names, Hawaii on its own and Cuiaba, were dropped: in the EDA hand check, at most 2 of every 10 emails using them were relevant.

Step 2: Two "nearby words" rules

A single word proves little. The word "guarantee" appears in 1,713 emails, mostly harmless. What matters is words appearing together. An email is flagged when, within about 200 words (roughly half a page):

  • Rule 1: a deal name (Group A) appears near any B, C or D word. Example: "Raptor" near "guarantee".
  • Rule 2: no deal name is needed, but words from all three of B, C and D appear together. Example: "repurchase" near "off-balance-sheet" near "quarter-end".

The rules are strict on purpose. Only 114 emails of 122,796 (under 0.1%) pass. Emails that pass go straight to the top of the list.

Step 3: TF-IDF scoring for everything else

Most relevant emails won't pass a strict rule, so every email also gets a similarity score from 0 to 1. Think of it like this:

We write a short "wanted poster" (the search text), and each email is scored on how much its vocabulary overlaps with it.

Rare words are worth more than common ones. Sharing the word "Raptor" (in fewer than 300 emails) earns far more points than sharing "the" or "meeting" (in tens of thousands). That is what the name means: term frequency (how often a word appears in this email) × inverse document frequency (how rare the word is across all emails).

The final top 1,000 is simple: first the rule matches, then the highest TF-IDF scores until the list is full. What goes into the search text turned out to matter a lot (section 4).

4. How we chose the search text

First version: the proposal's search text (Option 1)

The proposal says to use the 68 terms plus its descriptive paragraph about the kind of email we're looking for (the same paragraph the meaning search uses). We built that first, and found three problems:

  1. Ordinary words in the description added noise. Phrases like "that an", "internal business" and "then discusses" are rare in email, so the scoring treated them as strong clues. 190 of the 886 TF-IDF picks got in mostly because of them.
  2. Long emails won. With a 250-word search text, long emails that touch many of its words outscored short, pointed ones. The typical pick was about 8 times longer than a typical email.
  3. News digests crowded in. Daily "Enron Mentions" press clippings took 91 places. They're public news, not internal deal talk.

A fix we tried and rejected

In the Option 3 list, routine trading paperwork still fills the bottom places, driven by four generic terms: credit support, credit reserve, sign off, side letter. We also had the Roman numerals in "Raptor I/II/III/IV". We tried removing these five from the search text. It made things worse: known SPE emails found fell from 225 to 200 and press digests rose from 22 to 43. Other generic words (like "stock") took over their weight, and real "Raptor I" and "Raptor III" emails dropped out. So we kept the full word list.

Test 3: Does it find emails beyond the obvious names?

704 of the 1,000 emails never name an SPE. They use the language of the scheme (guarantees, write-downs, total return swaps, off-balance-sheet) without naming the partnership. A plain name search would never surface them. The labelled sample suggests many near the bottom are routine paperwork, which is why the combining step and human review come next.

Problems that remain

  • The bottom of the list is weak. Near the cut-off, many picks are routine trading paperwork (credit terms, guarantees, side letters) that share our vocabulary. Fusion with the meaning search gives these places little weight, and later review filters them out.
  • Some news digests are left: 22 "Enron Mentions" (was 91), 2 EnTouch newsletters (was 7), no Restricted List notices (was 9). Removing them from the starting pool would fix this for both searches. That's a group decision.

Glossary

SPE (special-purpose entity)
A separate company or partnership set up for one deal. Enron used SPEs such as Raptor and LJM to move assets and losses off its books, while often still guaranteeing them behind the scenes.
Lexical / keyword search
Searching by the actual words in an email, as opposed to "semantic" search, which compares meaning.
TF-IDF
A standard scoring method: a word counts more when it appears in this email (term frequency) and when it's rare across all emails (inverse document frequency).
Search text
The text TF-IDF compares every email against. Ours is the 68 Table 2 terms (Option 3).
Recall
Of the relevant emails that exist, what share our list caught.
Precision / hit rate
Of the emails on our list, what share are actually relevant. This is what the labelling will measure.
Top 1,000
The keyword channel's output: its best 1,000 candidate emails, which get combined with the meaning search's 1,000.
